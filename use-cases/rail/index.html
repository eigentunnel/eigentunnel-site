Use case · Rail

Secure links for wayside and remote sites, ready for quantum.

A railroad's data travels a long way: from wayside equipment and remote sites, over radio and cellular backhaul, through territory where coverage comes and goes. Those links drop. The equipment on them runs for decades. The encryption protecting them today will not hold against future quantum computers, and traffic recorded now can be decrypted later.

Why rail security teams can take a look

  • Patent pending (U.S. Application No. 64/175,794) for keeping secure links up on degraded networks.
  • SOF RACER member. Eigen-Systems, Inc. is a US-owned company in Omaha, Nebraska, and a member of the U.S. Special Operations Command's SOF RACER industry network.
  • Federal deadlines are set. NSA's CNSA 2.0 has VPNs and routers in National Security Systems supporting quantum-resistant algorithms by 2026 and using them exclusively by 2030 [1]. OMB M-26-15 put federal civilian agencies on a dated migration plan in 2026 [2]. These rules do not bind railroads directly, but they set the pace for the vendors and networks railroads depend on.

What EigenTunnel does for rail

  • Adds quantum-resistant encryption to a chosen connection, such as wayside to back office or a remote site to the network core.
  • Built to connect and stay connected on low-bandwidth links that drop and recover.
  • Works at the connection layer, so long-lived field equipment can stay in place while you plan its replacement.

What it is not

EigenTunnel is not part of any Positive Train Control (PTC) system and has not been reviewed by FRA. PTC communications have their own integrity and authentication requirements under 49 CFR 236.1033 [3], governed by each railroad's PTC safety plan. For context, FRA's PTC communications study reviews cryptographic options for constrained rail endpoints, including post-quantum options [4]. We suggest starting with non-safety data paths.

A small, low-risk next step

  1. A 20-minute look. One call about one link that gives you trouble. No deck, no data shared.
  2. A lab pilot. If it fits, test one connection in your lab or ours, with synthetic traffic and written acceptance criteria. Nothing touches operations.
