CNSA 2.0 for VPNs and tunnels, in plain language

If you run VPNs, site-to-site tunnels or routers for a National Security System, the clock on CNSA 2.0 is already running. This guide explains what CNSA 2.0 is, which dates apply to networking gear, which algorithms it names, and the questions to put to any tunnel vendor, including us.

What CNSA 2.0 is

CNSA 2.0 is the NSA's Commercial National Security Algorithm Suite 2.0: the set of public algorithms the NSA expects National Security Systems (NSS) to use as they move to quantum-resistant cryptography. It replaces CNSA 1.0 over a phased timeline by product category. The NSA expects the transition for NSS to be complete by 2035, in line with National Security Memorandum 10 (NSM-10). [1]

Why tunnels are near the front of the line

Adversaries can record encrypted traffic today and decrypt it later once a large quantum computer exists. This is often called "harvest now, decrypt later." VPNs and tunnels carry exactly the long-lived traffic worth recording, so the NSA puts traditional networking equipment on one of the earliest schedules. [1]

Trackers also report a January 1, 2027 procurement gate under CNSSP-15: new NSS acquisitions after that date are expected to support CNSA 2.0. [2] Confirm the current wording with your authorizing official, since this is where program schedules and buying decisions meet.

The algorithms, in one table

JobCNSA 2.0 algorithmWhat it replaces in a tunnel

Digital signaturesML-DSA-87 (FIPS 204)RSA or ECDSA authentication
Bulk encryptionAES-256Usually already in place
HashingSHA-384 or SHA-512Usually already in place
Firmware and software signingLMS or XMSSRSA or ECDSA code signing

Source: NSA CNSA 2.0 advisory. [1]

The symmetric parts of a tunnel are mostly fine already. The work is in the public-key parts: how the two ends agree on keys and how they prove who they are.

How the standards world is wiring this into tunnels

  • IPsec / IKEv2: RFC 9370 lets IKEv2 run more than one key exchange, so a post-quantum exchange such as ML-KEM can be added alongside a classical one. [3] Open-source strongSwan 6.0 added ML-KEM support using this approach. [4]
  • WireGuard-based tools: WireGuard intentionally avoids algorithm agility. Tailscale's documentation states its WireGuard implementation is not post-quantum secure today and describes pre-shared keys as the suggested path. [5]
  • Bigger keys, real consequences: post-quantum keys and signatures are much larger than the ones they replace. On clean networks that barely matters. On satellite, cellular IoT and radio links, larger handshakes mean fragmentation, retries and failed connections. Published research on low-Earth-orbit links measured exactly this effect. [6]

Questions to ask any tunnel vendor

  1. Which key-establishment and signature algorithms does the product use today, and can it run ML-KEM-1024 and ML-DSA-87 specifically?
  2. Is post-quantum key exchange hybrid (classical plus post-quantum) or post-quantum only, and is that configurable?
  3. What is the product's current validation and approval status for your environment? Ask for documents, not adjectives. For NSS, the NSA advisory explains that approval runs through NSA processes rather than FIPS validation alone. [1]
  4. How does the tunnel behave on your worst link: high loss, long delay, NAT, intermittent coverage?
  5. Can you swap algorithms later without replacing the equipment at each end?
  6. What evidence will you hand to the authorizing official at the end of a pilot?

A practical first step

Start with an inventory. You cannot plan a CNSA 2.0 migration for tunnels you have not found. Then pick one connection that matters, ideally the hardest one, and test a post-quantum path on it with clear acceptance criteria.
